# Nigeria's health-tech rivals leave trust unclaimed

Seven companies positioned against Tarragon Health in Nigeria's digital chronic-care market — Presibo, mDoc, Medlitics, MyMedicalBank, Avura Cares, WellNation Nigeria, and Mobihealth International — share a single, exploitable weakness: almost none of them has built a visible, independently verifiable trust footprint. Across all seven, researchers found no meaningful Trustpilot presence, app-store ratings that are either suppressed, absent, or based on samples in the single digits, and marketing copy that gestures at "trusted partners" and "verified practitioners" without naming a single hospital, lab, or insurer in five of the seven cases. Where competitors are strong, they are strong in narrow, learnable ways: Medlitics and mDoc write tight, condition-specific landing pages; Presibo and Avura Cares use founder story and local voice (including Nigerian Pidgin) to build emotional trust cheaply; mDoc and Medlitics both build real offline/low-bandwidth access (USSD, a PWA) that Tarragon's Next.js web app may not yet match. But every AI-forward competitor makes the same "augments doctors, never replaces them" claim without showing a concrete escalation mechanism — which is precisely what Tarragon already has, documented and database-enforced, and currently under-markets. The most capitalized rival, Mobihealth International, has raised institutional money at a scale (a ₦1 billion SCM Capital equity round, a $1.5 million Afreximbank facility, a $1 million USTDA grant) that dwarfs the rest of the field, yet shows almost no consumer review footprint either — proof that funding and reputation are not the same asset, and that Tarragon can compete on the latter without matching the former dollar for dollar. Two names in the brief require an upfront correction: "medilitics.com" is a parked domain with no operating company behind it, and the real subject is **Medlitics** (medlitics.com); "Avura Cares" and "WellNation Nigeria" both collide, in search results, with unrelated companies that share similar names and must not be conflated with the real subjects.

## Company profiles

### Presibo — mission-driven chronic care with a device bundle, but almost no public footprint

Presibo markets itself as an "AI-powered continuous chronic care platform," built around the tagline "**Continuous Chronic Care Management from home**" and a founder story: co-founder Jerry Nwobodo's aunt suffered an undetected hypertensive crisis in 2023, which the company cites as the reason "Nigerian healthcare was built for emergencies, not for the quiet daily work of managing chronic conditions" ([presibo.com/about](https://presibo.com/about/)). Its consumer offer bundles a connected BP cuff/glucometer, AI-generated insights, medication reminders, and in-app doctor chat/video consults across three named tiers — **Individual ₦5,000/month**, **Elder Care ₦15,000/month**, and **Family ₦25,000/month (up to 4 members)** — backed by a 30-day money-back guarantee ([presibo.com/subscribe](https://presibo.com/subscribe)). Notably, Presibo bundles a "Family portal" into even its base tier and runs dedicated Elder Care and Family plans, a direct product-scope contrast to Tarragon's individual-enrolment-only policy. Rather than customer logos, its homepage trust signals are entirely grant/accelerator badges — Tony Elumelu Foundation, Orange Corners, MIT Solve 2024 semifinalist, and Lagos State Employment Trust Fund ([presibo.com](https://presibo.com)) — consistent with a pre-institutional-funding, grant-stage company. A planned feature, "Presibo Flow AI," is described in press as supporting voice/text symptom triage in Yoruba, Igbo, and Hausa ([ThisDayLive](https://www.thisdaylive.com/2025/12/09/presibo-founder-tony-elumelu-foundations-5000-grant-recipient-jerry-nwobodo-accelerates-africas-digital-health-transformation-with-ai-powered-automation/)) — a genuinely differentiated idea, though unconfirmed as shipped in the current product. Presibo's actual market traction looks thin: its Android app ("Presibo GoTrack") has only seven visible reviews (all five-star) and a third-party aggregator puts installs at roughly 50 ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.presibotrack.app); [Liners.com](https://liners.com/presibo)), its iOS App Store link 404s, and — despite an MIT Solve semifinalist placement — no Techpoint Africa, TechCabal, Disrupt Africa, or Nairametrics coverage, LinkedIn company page, or Trustpilot listing could be found anywhere.

### mDoc (mymdoc.com) — the deepest-pedigreed rival, built deliberately without venture capital

mDoc, whose flagship product is **mDoc CompleteHealth™**, is a decade-plus-old (founded 2013) Lagos company built by CEO Dr. Nneka Mobisson — whose father, a prominent Nigerian technologist, died of a preventable stroke from uncontrolled hypertension in 2010, the company's own founding story ([Gates Foundation](https://www.gatesfoundation.org/ideas/articles/nneka-mobisson-mdoc-healthcare)) — and CTO/COO Imo Etuk. Its tagline, "**Your health in your hands. Anytime. Anywhere**" ([mymdoc.com/about-us](https://mymdoc.com/about-us)), sits atop a coach-led (not doctor-led) model: a 24-week "Propel Program," a personal health dashboard ("My Diary"), a longitudinal "Health Passport," and an AI chatbot named **Kem** (from the Igbo "nkem," "my own") that answers health questions in English and Nigerian Pidgin, with explicit human-in-the-loop escalation for sensitive cases ([Gates Foundation](https://www.gatesfoundation.org/ideas/articles/nneka-mobisson-mdoc-healthcare)). mDoc's real differentiator is structural: it runs four pillars — the app, physical "NudgeHub" community centers offering free BP/glucose checks, a geo-coded provider directory ("NaviHealth.ai") verified against Nigeria's Medical Laboratory Science Council, and a tele-education arm ("MQN") reaching 25 African countries — and explicitly supports USSD access for feature phones, extending reach well beyond smartphone owners ([AI World](https://aiworld.eu/story/mdoc-provides-ai-driven-disease-support-across-nigeria)). mDoc reports **150,000+ members**, disproportionately women earning under $2/day ([Yale Insights](https://insights.som.yale.edu/insights/building-for-profit-health-model-that-reaches-the-poorest)), and its founder is on record deliberately avoiding VC to preserve mission focus, instead layering grant funding (Gates Foundation, Google for Startups, MassChallenge — a disclosed total that conflicting trackers put at $148K–$248K) under real revenue lines: consumer subscriptions, employer contracts, and — notably — **B2G capitation fees from state health-insurance plans**, a monetization path Tarragon's own operating rules explicitly and permanently rule out. Despite this pedigree, mDoc's Android-only app (10K+ downloads) shows **no visible star rating or review section at all** on its live Play Store listing, and no Trustpilot, Nairaland, or Reddit discussion of the company could be found anywhere — a striking mismatch between institutional credibility and public consumer-review visibility.

### Medlitics (medlitics.com) — a sharp, very new AI-monitoring pitch built on USD pricing and no app

The domain named in the original brief, **medilitics.com**, does not resolve to an operating company — it redirects to a GoDaddy "domain for sale" page — and a second, unrelated company ("Medilitics Connect," a B2B FHIR-conversion tool with no Nigerian ties) also exists under a similar name. The real subject matching every element of the brief is **Medlitics** (medlitics.com, "Medlitics Limited"), a very recently launched (2026) platform run by serial entrepreneur Michael Fasere, previously founder of fashion-tech platform Pashione and co-founder of fintech platform 1App Technologies ([Techeconomy.ng](https://techeconomy.ng/a-chat-with-michael-fasere-on-how-medlitics-is-using-ai-and-offline-first-tech-to-fix-africas-chronic-disease-crisis)). Medlitics runs the sharpest, most conversion-optimized marketing site of the seven: a hero built on hard numbers ("**Better chronic disease management. Save 50% on care**," "prevent stroke, reduce risk by 78%"), condition-specific sales funnels for diabetes/hypertension/asthma, and paired patient-plus-doctor testimonials with named city/condition attribution — e.g., "Medlitics caught my blood pressure spiking before I even felt symptoms. My doctor was already calling when I picked up my phone" ([medlitics.com](https://www.medlitics.com/)). Its AI assistant, "Meddy," and wearable sync (Fitbit, Samsung Health, Google Fit, Apple Health, plus Bluetooth BP/glucometers, syncing every 15 minutes per press coverage) closely parallel Tarragon's own wearables pipeline ([BusinessDay NG](https://businessday.ng/news/article/african-health-startup-targets-diabetes-hypertension-costs-with-ai-powered-monitoring-platform/)). Two choices stand out as real strategic risks worth naming: pricing is denominated entirely in **USD** ($9.99/$19.99 per month, plus couple and corporate tiers) in a Naira retail market ([medlitics.com/pricing](https://www.medlitics.com/pricing)), and Medlitics deliberately ships **no native iOS or Android app at all**, betting entirely on an installable Progressive Web App for offline-first, low-bandwidth reach ([Techeconomy.ng](https://techeconomy.ng/a-chat-with-michael-fasere-on-how-medlitics-is-using-ai-and-offline-first-tech-to-fix-africas-chronic-disease-crisis)) — a real technical bet, but one that forgoes app-store discovery and social proof entirely. Payments route through the founder's own separate fintech venture (`checkout.1app.online`) rather than a standard Nigerian gateway, a related-party detail worth flagging. No funding, no named hospital or lab partner (only insurance-tech vendor Curacel), and no third-party reviews of any kind were found anywhere.

### MyMedicalBank — a broad, older-stack healthcare marketplace with real HMO integration but thin reputation

Despite its name, MyMedicalBank is not primarily a personal-health-record vault; the live site foregrounds a **healthcare marketplace/booking platform** — "Nigeria's #1 Digital Health Platform," offering "Doctors, nurses, carers, lab tests, physiotherapy, screenings, dental care and more" across home visits, clinic visits, and telemedicine ([mymedicalbank.com](https://www.mymedicalbank.com/)). Founded in the UK in 2014 and in Nigeria in 2016 by CEO Ayo Adebamowo, a former NHS senior health informatics manager, the company runs at least nine named product components per its own brochure — MMB HomeCare, Health Market, Health Jobs, a PHR/EMR hybrid, a facility directory, and more — and, notably, has a **real, live, bookable HMO integration**: REDCARE HMO's "R-Care" and "R-Care Plus" plans are directly purchasable on the platform ([mymedicalbank.com/service/R-Care](https://www.mymedicalbank.com/service/R-Care-%28Health-Insurance-Plan%29/135)). Its 2026-era **AI Health Assistant** lets users upload lab results (PDF, image, or document) for plain-language explanation, then hands off to a named WhatsApp persona ("Chat with Sharon on WhatsApp 24/7") before upselling a paid doctor consult from the same screen ([resources.mymedicalbank.com](https://resources.mymedicalbank.com/ai-health-assistant-nigeria/)) — an effective top-of-funnel lead-generation pattern. Its live pricing page (per-service, e.g., a doctor home visit at ₦40,000/hour) diverges 5–10x from its own older brochure's figures for equivalent line items, an internal inconsistency the report treats as a red flag about document hygiene rather than a resolved fact. Technically, the site runs on a **PHP/Fat-Free Framework backend with jQuery/Bootstrap-era front-end tooling** and full-session-recording analytics via Mixpanel — a materially older architecture than Tarragon's Next.js/Supabase stack. Its iOS app carries a 4.0-star rating from just three visible ratings, and the only substantive negative review found describes being blocked from completing onboarding by a mandatory, apparently non-functional birthday/genotype field ("the app doesn't even let me type or input anything so I'm stuck") ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/ng/app/mymedicalbank/id6464156472)) — a concrete onboarding-friction failure worth checking Tarragon's own onboarding against.

### Avura Cares — a warm, Pidgin-voiced brand with almost no discoverable operation behind it

Avura Cares presents itself as a "preventive healthcare-tech company" with a hero line written entirely in Nigerian Pidgin — "**No wait make body knock ground — Prevention still better pass cure**" ([avuracares.com](https://www.avuracares.com/)) — paired with a flat, simple **₦5,000/month** entry price for "basic telemedicine access." Its service list spans outpatient clinics, domiciliary (home-based) care, a bundled "Health Insurance" plan of unstated underwriting, nutritional profiling, ambulance support, and mental-health/physiotherapy sessions, but the site is a single scrolling page — `/about`, `/services`, and `/pricing` all 404 as separate URLs — signaling an early-stage, lean marketing footprint rather than a content-rich product site. No mobile app could be found on either store under this name (searches surfaced only unrelated apps like "AuraCare" and "Auro Cares"), no funding or press coverage exists in any outlet searched, and no Trustpilot, Google Reviews, or forum discussion could be found. The one identifiable team member, Anthony Mabiaku, appears on LinkedIn as a biomedical engineer/business-development professional, with no confirmed founder title. A real hazard for anyone researching this company: a completely unrelated retail brand also named "Avura" (ozone-sanitization products, avura.com/avuralife.com) repeatedly surfaces in searches and must not be conflated with Avura Cares. On present evidence, Avura Cares reads as the least mature of the seven competitors by almost every measurable signal — but its Pidgin-language, locally-relatable brand voice is itself a genuinely useful data point on tone, independent of the company's scale.

### WellNation Nigeria — a broker/integrator model with no named partners and an unresolved corporate structure

WellNation Nigeria positions itself not as a care provider but as a **"healthcare ecosystem integrator"** — "**Empowering Nigeria's Health Ecosystem**," a "unified, technology-driven healthcare platform connecting wellness, diagnostics, telehealth, pharmacy, and insurance through trusted national partnerships" ([wellnationnigeria.com](https://wellnationnigeria.com)). Every page repeats an explicit disclaimer that the company does **not** provide medical diagnosis, treatment, laboratory testing, or insurance underwriting directly — those are delegated to unnamed third-party "licensed" practitioners, labs, and insurers. This is the most clearly non-vertically-integrated model among the seven, a direct structural contrast to Tarragon's employed-doctor, own-the-clinical-relationship approach. The site itself is a lean, five-page brochure with **no pricing page, no product screenshots, no testimonials, and no named partner logos anywhere** — and no evidence exists that WellNation Nigeria has its own dedicated app; the only discoverable "Well Nation"-branded apps ("Well Nation Africa"/"WNAfrica") belong to a South Africa-based sister brand centered on a wearables-and-gamified-challenges corporate-wellness product, and Apple's own listing states the app "hasn't received enough ratings or reviews to display an overview." The precise corporate relationship between wellnationnigeria.com, wellnationafrica.com, and wellnation.com (which shares its contact-email domain) was never confirmed by any source and should be treated as an open question, not a settled fact. No funding, press coverage, named partners, founder identity, or third-party reviews of any kind were found for the Nigeria-specific entity — making it, alongside Avura Cares, one of the two lowest-visibility competitors in this set.

### Mobihealth International — the best-capitalized rival, with institutional credibility far outpacing consumer-review visibility

Mobihealth is the outlier of the seven on scale and capital: founded in 2017 by Dr. Funmi Adewara, a Nigerian-British doctor with 15 years in the UK's NHS, the company has assembled a genuinely large institutional backing stack — a **₦1 billion equity investment from SCM Capital** (2026, structured by United Capital) ([BusinessDay NG](https://businessday.ng/health/article/mobihealth-raises-n1bn-equity-investment-to-push-telehealth-expansion-in-nigeria/)), a **$1 million USTDA grant** (2022) to study expansion into Côte d'Ivoire, Ghana, Kenya, and Egypt ([USTDA](https://www.ustda.gov/ustda-mobihealth-partner-on-african-telehealth-infrastructure/)), and a **$1.5 million Afreximbank Project Preparation Facility** (2024) explicitly aimed at unlocking an eventual $65 million in further investment ([Afreximbank](https://www.afreximbank.com/afreximbank-and-mobihealth-sign-project-preparation-facility-to-drive-digital-healthcare-solutions-across-africa/)). Its go-to-market spans B2C, B2B, B2G, and SaaS/white-label motions, headlined by hard numbers — "**75% Cost Savings**," hospital congestion "reduced by over 60%," access to "**100,000+ global medical experts**" — and physical infrastructure: 20 solar/satellite-powered "telehealth clinics" and mobile clinics layered atop the consultation app. A November 2024 partnership embeds Mobihealth directly inside the **Airtel Nigeria app**, offering subscribers 24/7 doctor access "from ₦5,000 per year" — a telco-distribution channel none of the other six competitors have. Yet this institutional weight has not translated into visible consumer trust: Mobihealth's app presence is fragmented across at least six listings (patient, doctor, an older "Consult" app, and a white-labeled "Fluvina" variant), none of the currently-live listings show a meaningful public rating, and no Trustpilot, Google Reviews, Nairaland, or Reddit presence exists anywhere — the starkest top-down-credibility-versus-bottom-up-consumer-proof gap of any competitor studied, and a direct illustration of the report's central finding.

## Marketing copy worth adapting

**Trust-building without institutional logos.** mDoc's disclaimer-forward "self-care support, not a substitute for... treatment" framing ([Google Play listing](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.mDoc.completehealth)) mirrors Tarragon's own discipline of describing "clinical review and escalation" rather than overclaiming — proof the market rewards precise, non-inflated clinical-scope language rather than punishing it. Presibo's founder-story approach (a family member's undetected hypertensive crisis, told in first person on the About page) converts an abstract mission statement into a specific, memorable stake, at zero production cost — worth adapting into Tarragon's own "why we exist" copy if a comparably concrete, real story exists to tell honestly.

**Quiet urgency, not fear.** Presibo's problem-framing line — "**Most strokes and diabetes complications happen because people go months without checking their numbers**" ([presibo.com](https://presibo.com)) — states a real consequence without alarmist language, exactly the register Tarragon's brand guide already calls for. Medlitics's testimonial pattern goes further and dramatizes a near-miss caught in time ("My doctor was already calling when I picked up my phone") — a format worth adapting (a real, consented patient story of "we caught it before it became an emergency") without adopting Medlitics's blunter percentage-claim headlines ("reduce risk by 78%"), which read closer to the "fear-based urgency" Tarragon's brand guide explicitly avoids.

**Simplicity and plain language.** MyMedicalBank's AI-assistant landing page names concrete Nigerian frictions instead of generic benefits — "**No queue. No Lagos traffic. No confusing medical jargon**" ([resources.mymedicalbank.com](https://resources.mymedicalbank.com/ai-health-assistant-nigeria/)) — a pattern of naming the exact local pain point (traffic, queueing, jargon) rather than a generic "convenient healthcare" claim. Avura Cares' single flat ₦5,000/month price, stated once and plainly, removes the tier-comparison cognitive load that a multi-tier pricing table (Presibo, Medlitics) imposes — worth remembering for any Tarragon pricing page redesign aimed at first-time, price-sensitive visitors.

**Pricing clarity.** Presibo's tiered pricing page names exactly what each tier includes line-by-line and backs it with a **30-day money-back guarantee** ([presibo.com/subscribe](https://presibo.com/subscribe)) — a concrete risk-reversal mechanic Tarragon could adapt (e.g., a satisfaction guarantee on the first chronic-care programme cycle) without reopening its own subscription-model decisions. Medlitics's pricing table is transparent and well-laid-out in structure — free tier, feature-gated paid tiers, clear group/corporate pricing — even though its USD denomination is a genuine strategic misstep in this market; the structural clarity (not the currency) is what's worth copying.

**Local-language and cultural resonance.** Avura Cares' Pidgin-language hero copy and mDoc's Kem chatbot (named from the Igbo "nkem," responding in Nigerian Pidgin) both demonstrate that even a single well-chosen local-language touch — a headline, a chatbot's name and register — reads as authenticity rather than gimmick in this market. Presibo's announced (though unconfirmed-as-shipped) Yoruba/Igbo/Hausa voice-triage concept is the most ambitious version of this pattern found across all seven competitors, and remains open territory: no competitor in this set has a *confirmed, live* multilingual product feature beyond mDoc's Pidgin chatbot responses.

**Social proof formatting.** Medlitics's testimonial cards pair a patient quote with a doctor quote and attribute both to a named city and condition ("Funmi Olatunji, Patient · Lagos · Hypertension"; "Dr. Emeka Adeola, Cardiologist · Abuja") — a two-sided-trust format (patient relief + clinician confidence, both geographically anchored across Nigeria's major cities) worth adapting directly, provided any testimonials Tarragon uses are genuinely sourced and consented, unlike the unverifiable, company-published testimonials found across nearly every competitor in this set.

## Functionality worth adopting

- **Condition-specific landing pages.** mDoc and Medlitics both build dedicated pages per condition (diabetes, hypertension, asthma) rather than one generic product pitch — each can carry its own risk-education stat, its own testimonial, and its own SEO target. Tarragon's marketing site (`apps/web/src/app/(marketing)/`) could add a small number of these for its own core wedge conditions without any backend change.
- **AI-assisted lab-result and symptom explainer with a governed human handoff.** MyMedicalBank's "upload your result, get a plain-language explanation, then book a doctor from the same screen" flow and Medlitics's "Meddy" assistant are both compelling top-of-funnel patterns. Any Tarragon equivalent must be built as a registered AI system routed through `runGovernedAi()` per `docs/AI_GOVERNANCE_SPEC.md` — this is exactly the kind of call site the registry exists for, and none of the competitors studied show evidence of an equivalent formal governance layer, which is a genuine point of technical superiority worth preserving rather than shortcutting.
- **A verified, named provider/lab directory with user feedback.** mDoc's NaviHealth.ai — geo-coded, feedback-enabled, and cross-checked against Nigeria's Medical Laboratory Science Council register — is a credible pattern to study against Tarragon's own lab-location picker (`list_lab_test_locations`), which currently has no user-review layer.
- **Low-bandwidth/offline resilience.** Medlitics's offline-first PWA design and mDoc's USSD support for feature phones both reflect real Nigerian connectivity constraints that a pure smartphone-app strategy can miss. Tarragon should evaluate whether its Next.js web app has an adequate offline/poor-connectivity story (service worker, graceful degradation) as a genuine product gap check, separate from any USSD investment (which would need careful scoping against the "WhatsApp/SMS notification-only, never a transactional interface" rule — a USSD channel could plausibly be built as a read-only status/reminder surface without violating that rule, but should not become an entry point for logging clinical data).
- **A risk-reversal offer on the first paid engagement.** Presibo's 30-day money-back guarantee is a low-cost trust mechanic. A Tarragon analog — e.g., a satisfaction guarantee on the first month of the 12-week doctor-supported chronic-care programme, or a no-questions-asked refund on a first one-off consult — could reduce first-purchase friction without touching the pricing model itself.
- **Telco/partner distribution bundling.** Mobihealth's embedded-in-Airtel-app distribution is a genuinely different acquisition channel from anything the other six competitors (or Tarragon today) use — worth a scoping conversation, even if Tarragon's own no-capitation, no-family-plan discipline means any such deal would need careful structuring to avoid the same B2G/insurer entanglement risk mDoc has taken on.

## Backend/technical patterns worth adopting

Relative to the seven competitors studied, Tarragon's Next.js 16 + Supabase/Postgres-with-RLS + `ai_systems`/`runGovernedAi()` governance stack is more architecturally rigorous than anything found in public signals from any rival — none show evidence of row-level multi-tenant isolation, a formal AI-system registry, an escalation-SLA data model, or a doctor-authority ladder enforced at the database layer. That said, three narrower technical patterns are worth deliberately evaluating rather than dismissing:

First, **Medlitics's offline-first Progressive Web App** approach is a genuine, deliberate bet on Nigerian connectivity realities, explicitly designed to avoid app-store friction and 30% platform fees while working in low-bandwidth conditions ([Techeconomy.ng](https://techeconomy.ng/a-chat-with-michael-fasere-on-how-medlitics-is-using-ai-and-offline-first-tech-to-fix-africas-chronic-disease-crisis)) — Tarragon should audit its own web app's behavior on a slow or intermittent connection, since Medlitics's choice implies this is a real, felt problem in the target market, even though going PWA-only (forgoing native apps entirely, as Medlitics has) would be the wrong lesson to draw given Tarragon's existing, working React Native mobile app.

Second, **mDoc's USSD gateway integration** for feature-phone reach is a real technical pattern (an SMS/USSD-gateway integration, not a new finding for Tarragon's existing Termii relationship) worth exploring specifically as a **read-only status-check or reminder surface** — consistent with Tarragon's own rule that WhatsApp/SMS may remind or notify but never serve as a transactional entry point — if reaching non-smartphone users becomes a stated priority.

Third, and as a caution rather than a recommendation: **MyMedicalBank's use of full-session-recording analytics (Mixpanel, `record_sessions_percent: 100`)** demonstrates real investment in conversion-funnel visibility, but recording full sessions on a platform that also handles clinical data carries privacy exposure Tarragon should not replicate uncritically — any session-recording tooling considered for Tarragon's own marketing/onboarding funnel should be scoped tightly away from any authenticated, PHI-adjacent surface.

Beyond these three, the competitive set offers little else to emulate technically: several sites show signs of unsettled tooling (Medlitics runs three overlapping chat widgets — Intercom, Crisp, and Tawk.to — simultaneously permitted in its CSP header, suggesting an in-progress vendor migration rather than a stable choice), MyMedicalBank runs on a materially older PHP/jQuery-era stack than a modern SPA, and no competitor publishes an engineering blog, public API documentation, or GitHub organization — Tarragon's own relative technical opacity to outsiders is therefore an industry norm, not a gap unique to Tarragon.

## What competitors do well

Synthesizing across all seven, four real strengths recur. **Concrete, benefit-first headline copy** — a cost-savings percentage, a risk-reduction percentage, a response-time figure — appears in Presibo, Medlitics, and Mobihealth's hero sections, and reads as more credible to a skeptical Nigerian consumer than an abstract mission statement alone, even where the underlying statistic is self-reported and unaudited. **Two-sided testimonial pairing** (a patient's relief alongside a doctor's operational confidence, both geographically anchored) appears cleanly in Medlitics and, less formally, in Presibo and MyMedicalBank, and is a genuinely effective trust format worth reusing with real, consented sources. **Local-connectivity realism** — mDoc's USSD support, Medlitics's offline-first PWA — shows two of the seven companies have thought seriously about Nigeria's actual device and bandwidth distribution rather than assuming smartphone-and-broadband ubiquity. And **every AI-forward competitor (mDoc, Medlitics, Presibo) is careful to state, explicitly and repeatedly, that its AI "augments" or "assists" clinicians rather than replacing them** — a shared, market-validated positioning discipline that happens to match Tarragon's own AI-governance posture almost exactly, which is worth taking as confirmation that this framing is doing real trust-building work in the category, not just internal risk management.

## What competitors do poorly

The dominant, category-wide failure is **an almost total absence of independent, verifiable reputation signal**. Not one of the seven companies has a populated Trustpilot page; app-store ratings are either literally absent (Medlitics, Avura Cares, WellNation Nigeria — no app exists), suppressed by the platform's own minimum-review threshold despite meaningful download counts (mDoc's 10K+ downloads with zero visible rating), or drawn from samples too small to mean anything (Presibo's 7 reviews, MyMedicalBank's 3 ratings). Even the best-funded competitor, Mobihealth International, with a ₦1 billion raise and years of press coverage, shows no Trustpilot, Nairaland, or Reddit presence and app listings with statistically meaningless ratings — the single clearest illustration that institutional credibility and consumer-review credibility are two entirely separate assets in this market, and that none of the seven has built the second one.

A second recurring failure is **vague or unnamed partnerships presented as trust signals**. WellNation Nigeria's entire pitch rests on "trusted national partnerships" while naming zero labs, HMOs, or pharmacy chains anywhere on its site; Avura Cares' "Health Insurance" service line names no underwriter; Medlitics names exactly one partner (insurance-tech vendor Curacel) and no hospitals or labs. By contrast, MyMedicalBank and Presibo at least name specific facilities or grant bodies, even if thinly evidenced. This is a real opening: Tarragon already has named, documented lab and HMO relationships (Synlab Nigeria, Reliance, Avon, Ronsberger, Wellahealth), and loudly featuring real named partners is rare enough in this category to function as a credibility differentiator on its own.

A third failure is **pricing and documentation inconsistency**, which reads as low operational rigor to a careful observer even when not consumer-facing: MyMedicalBank's brochure and live pricing page diverge 5–10x on equivalent line items; Presibo's own homepage misstates the currency of its Tony Elumelu Foundation grant against consistent press reporting; Medlitics runs three chat-widget vendors simultaneously permitted in its security headers, a sign of unfinished vendor migration. None of these is individually damning, but together they show a category where marketing surfaces are not tightly maintained — a bar Tarragon's own CLAUDE.md-documented discipline around stale-claim correction and drift tracking already clears.

Two additional, more strategic weaknesses are worth naming directly. **USD-denominated consumer pricing in a Naira retail market (Medlitics)** creates FX-volatility risk and cognitive friction for a price-sensitive buyer — a mistake Tarragon's kobo-precise, NGN-first pricing model structurally avoids. And **B2G capitation revenue (mDoc)** ties a chronic-care platform's incentives to insurer/government payer relationships in ways that can misalign with patient-first care — precisely the category of business-model risk Tarragon's founder has permanently and explicitly ruled out (I8, "no capitation, ever"). Both are worth stating plainly in Tarragon's own positioning as deliberate discipline rather than mere omission.

## How Tarragon can outcompete and stand out

1. **Claim the reputation vacuum first.** No competitor in this category has real, visible third-party social proof. Build a systematic post-interaction review-request flow (after a resolved escalation, a clean lab result, or a completed chronic-care check-in), get onto Trustpilot with genuine reviews, and treat App Store/Play Store rating volume as a tracked growth metric — this is the single most defensible, lowest-cost differentiator available, precisely because it requires patient trust the competitors haven't earned rather than capital they mostly have more of.

2. **Turn documented clinical rigor into honest, specific marketing claims.** Every AI-forward competitor claims "AI augments doctors, never replaces them," but none show a concrete mechanism. Tarragon actually has one — a database-enforced escalation SLA, a tiered doctor-authority ladder, and an `ai_systems` governance registry — and can say, plainly and without a specific unconfirmed ratio (per its own brand-copy rule), that every case is reviewed against a defined process by an employed, MDCN-registered clinician. This is a genuine, provable claim none of the seven competitors can currently make with equal specificity.

3. **Message NGN-first pricing as a deliberate trust decision, not just a default.** Medlitics's USD pricing and MyMedicalBank's inconsistent price lists are real, citable competitor missteps. A short, explicit "always priced in Naira, one price list, no surprise FX conversion" message directly targets a felt pain point this research surfaced twice.

4. **Adopt the condition-specific landing-page pattern, in Tarragon's own voice.** Build dedicated hypertension and diabetes pages carrying a quiet-urgency statistic (in the register of Presibo's "most complications happen because people go months without checking their numbers," not Medlitics's blunter percentage claims) alongside a real, consented two-sided testimonial pair — this directly serves both SEO and message-market fit.

5. **Investigate a genuine local-language support option.** No competitor in this set has a *confirmed, shipped* multilingual triage feature beyond mDoc's Pidgin-capable chatbot; Presibo's announced Yoruba/Igbo/Hausa ambition is unconfirmed as live. This is open, differentiated territory given Tarragon's stronger engineering base — worth a scoping conversation as a deliberate roadmap item, built (like any AI call site) through the governed-AI registry.

6. **Feature real named partners loudly.** Five of the seven competitors either name no partners or name only one thin vendor relationship. Tarragon's actual, documented lab and HMO relationships are a rare, credible asset in this category and should be visually and textually prominent on the marketing site, not buried.

7. **Audit low-bandwidth resilience.** Two credible competitors (mDoc via USSD, Medlitics via an offline-first PWA) have made real, deliberate bets on Nigeria's connectivity constraints. Tarragon should confirm its own web app degrades gracefully on a poor connection, and evaluate — cautiously, and only as a read-only reminder/status surface consistent with the existing WhatsApp/SMS notification-only rule — whether a USSD channel is worth adding for reach into non-smartphone segments.

8. **State the two guardrails Tarragon already holds as deliberate strategic discipline, not silence.** "No capitation, ever" (unlike mDoc) and "one Naira price list, no USD-denominated consumer pricing" (unlike Medlitics) are both real, citable competitor weaknesses Tarragon has already structurally avoided — worth a line in founder-facing and even public materials framing these as considered choices rather than simply the default.

9. **Consider a risk-reversal offer on first paid engagement**, modeled on Presibo's 30-day money-back guarantee, scoped to Tarragon's actual product (e.g., a satisfaction guarantee on the first chronic-care programme cycle or a refund on a first one-off consult) — a low-cost trust mechanic that doesn't require reopening any pricing-model decision.

10. **Do not reopen the individual-enrolment-only decision, but do note the demand signal.** Presibo's bundled Family/Elder-care tiers show real market appetite for caregiver-inclusive plans. The safer response, consistent with Tarragon's founder-narrowed product scope, is to make the existing `profile_access`/caregiver consent-graph model more visible and better-explained in marketing and onboarding copy — capturing the same trust benefit without reopening a settled business-model decision.

## Conclusion

The clearest strategic implication of this research is that Tarragon Health is not behind this field on the dimensions that actually determine long-term category leadership — clinical governance, database-enforced escalation discipline, NGN-first pricing hygiene, and named partner relationships are all areas where Tarragon's documented internal discipline already exceeds anything visible in these seven competitors' public materials. What Tarragon has not yet done is *convert that discipline into visible proof* the way a skeptical Nigerian consumer actually evaluates a health platform: through reviews, ratings, and named social proof, none of which any competitor in this set has meaningfully built either. That makes the review and reputation gap the single highest-leverage opportunity in this analysis — not because it requires new capital or new clinical infrastructure, but because it requires only that Tarragon systematically ask satisfied patients to say so publicly, in a market where literally none of its seven closest rivals has done that at scale.
