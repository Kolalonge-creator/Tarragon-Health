## 1. Cholesterol results, decoded

**Slug:** `cholesterol-explained`
**Title:** What is a normal cholesterol level? Your cholesterol test results explained
**Description:** What total cholesterol, LDL, HDL and triglycerides mean, typical healthy ranges in mmol/L and mg/dL, and when cholesterol needs treating.

### What does a cholesterol test measure?
A cholesterol test, often called a lipid profile, usually reports four numbers:
- Total cholesterol: all the cholesterol in your blood added together.
- LDL cholesterol: the kind that, in excess, builds up in artery walls. It is the one treatment usually aims to lower.
- HDL cholesterol: the kind that carries cholesterol away from the arteries. Higher is generally better.
- Triglycerides: a type of fat in the blood that rises with sugar, alcohol and excess weight.

### Do I need to fast before a cholesterol test?
Usually not. A non-fasting sample gives reliable results for most people. Your doctor may ask you to fast if your triglycerides were very high before, or if the laboratory asks for it.

### What is a normal cholesterol level?
There is no single "normal" for everyone, because what matters is your overall risk of heart attack and stroke. As a general guide, laboratories commonly use these healthy ranges for adults: **[CHECK all values]**
- Total cholesterol: 5.0 mmol/L or less (about 193 mg/dL or less).
- LDL cholesterol: 3.0 mmol/L or less (about 116 mg/dL or less).
- HDL cholesterol: 1.0 mmol/L or more for men and 1.2 mmol/L or more for women (about 39 and 46 mg/dL).
- Triglycerides: below 1.7 mmol/L when fasting (about 150 mg/dL), or below 2.3 mmol/L when not fasting (about 200 mg/dL).
Nigerian laboratories may report in mg/dL or mmol/L; the result sheet says which.

### What is non-HDL cholesterol?
Subtract HDL from total cholesterol and you get non-HDL cholesterol: all the cholesterol that can clog arteries, in one number. Many doctors now find it a better single measure than LDL alone, and it needs no extra test.

### When does high cholesterol need treatment?
It depends on your overall risk, not on one number. Your doctor estimates your chance of a heart attack or stroke over the next ten years, using your age, sex, blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, cholesterol and family history. The same LDL can be acceptable in one person and need treatment in someone who has diabetes or has already had a heart problem. People with diabetes, kidney disease or existing heart disease are often advised to take a statin regardless of their cholesterol level. **[CHECK]**

### Can high cholesterol run in families?
Yes. A very high result, for example a total cholesterol above 7.5 mmol/L (about 290 mg/dL), especially with a parent or sibling who had a heart attack young, can be a sign of familial hypercholesterolaemia, an inherited condition. It needs a doctor's assessment, and close relatives should be tested too. **[CHECK]**

### How can I lower my cholesterol?
- Eat less fried food, fatty meat and processed snacks, and more beans, oats, vegetables, fruit and fish.
- Cook with smaller amounts of oil, and avoid reusing deep-frying oil many times.
- Be active most days, and lose weight if you carry extra.
- Stop smoking and keep alcohol low; both affect HDL and triglycerides.
For people at meaningful risk, statins such as atorvastatin are among the best-studied medicines there are, and they lower the risk of heart attack and stroke. Your doctor will discuss whether one is right for you.

### How often should I check my cholesterol?
Many adults are first offered a lipid profile from around age 40, or earlier with risk factors such as diabetes, high blood pressure or a family history. If it is normal and your risk is low, it is usually repeated every few years. People on a statin or with diabetes are usually checked about once a year. **[CHECK]**

### Guidelines this article follows
NICE guideline NG238: Cardiovascular disease, risk assessment and reduction, including lipid modification. NICE guideline CG71: Familial hypercholesterolaemia. Tarragon's clinical pathways, based on WHO cardiovascular risk guidance; there is no Nigerian national guideline on cholesterol levels.
Where Nigerian national guidance and NICE differ, this article follows the Nigerian guideline, which is also what Tarragon's care team uses.

## 2. Prediabetes: the warning stage you can still reverse

**Slug:** `prediabetes-the-reversible-warning-stage`
**Title:** Prediabetes: what the numbers mean and how to reverse it
**Description:** What prediabetes is, the blood sugar and HbA1c ranges that define it, and the changes that bring it back to normal.

### What is prediabetes?
Prediabetes means your blood sugar is higher than normal but not yet high enough to be called diabetes. It is an early warning that often shows up years before diabetes would. It usually causes no symptoms, which is why it is found by a blood test.

### What blood sugar and HbA1c levels mean prediabetes?
Using Nigeria's national diabetes guidelines: **[CHECK]**
- HbA1c (the 3-month sugar test): 5.7% to 6.4% (39 to 47 mmol/mol).
- Fasting blood sugar: 5.6 to 6.9 mmol/L (100 to 125 mg/dL).
- Two-hour glucose tolerance test: 7.8 to 11.0 mmol/L (140 to 199 mg/dL).
Above these ranges, a test suggests diabetes, usually confirmed with a second test. In the UK, NICE uses a slightly higher HbA1c starting point of 6.0%; Tarragon follows the Nigerian guideline.

### Can prediabetes be reversed?
Often, yes. Many people who lose a modest amount of weight and become more active bring their numbers back into the normal range. Without changes, many people with prediabetes go on to develop type 2 diabetes over several years. That is why this stage is the best time to act: it is reversal now, rather than management later.

### What changes work best?
- Losing 5% to 10% of your body weight, for example 4 to 8 kg for someone who weighs 80 kg.
- At least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, such as 30 minutes of brisk walking on five days.
- Cutting out sugary drinks, including sweetened zobo, malt and soft drinks.
- More vegetables, beans and whole grains, and smaller portions of swallow, rice and white bread.
- Not smoking, and enough sleep.
**[CHECK]**

### Will I need medicine for prediabetes?
Most people do not. Lifestyle change is the first treatment. For people at higher risk, for example with a BMI of 35 or more, those under 60, or women who had diabetes in a past pregnancy, a doctor may consider metformin alongside lifestyle change, especially if the numbers are not improving. **[CHECK]**

### How often should I be retested?
Usually once a year, to see which direction your numbers are moving. Sooner if you develop symptoms such as unusual thirst, passing more urine or weight loss without trying.

### Who should get tested?
The same people who should be tested for diabetes: anyone with a parent or sibling with diabetes, extra weight around the waist, age 40 or over, high blood pressure, diabetes in a past pregnancy, polycystic ovary syndrome, or a previous borderline reading.

### Guidelines this article follows
Federal Ministry of Health, Nigeria: National Guidelines on the Prevention, Control and Management of Diabetes Mellitus. Diabetes Association of Nigeria: National Clinical Practice Guidelines for Diabetes Management in Nigeria. NICE guideline PH38: Type 2 diabetes, prevention in people at high risk.
Where Nigerian national guidance and NICE differ, this article follows the Nigerian guideline, which is also what Tarragon's care team uses.

## 3. Is my weight a health problem? BMI, waist size, and what helps

**Slug:** `weight-bmi-waist`
**Title:** BMI and waist size: how to tell if your weight is a health risk
**Description:** How to work out your BMI, healthy ranges, why waist size and waist-to-height ratio matter, and what actually helps.

### How do I work out my BMI?
BMI (body mass index) is your weight in kilograms divided by your height in metres, squared. For example, someone who weighs 80 kg and is 1.70 m tall has a BMI of 80 divided by 2.89, which is about 27.7. You can also use the BMI calculator on our website.

### What is a healthy BMI?
For adults, using the WHO ranges that Nigerian health services use: **[CHECK]**
- Below 18.5: underweight.
- 18.5 to 24.9: healthy weight.
- 25 to 29.9: overweight.
- 30 or more: obesity.
NICE in the UK suggests lower cut-offs for people of Black African family origin, because health risks start at a lower BMI: overweight from 23 and obesity from 27.5. Tarragon uses the WHO ranges, but your doctor will look at your waist size and other risks as well, so a BMI just under 25 is not automatically reassuring.

### Why does waist size matter?
Fat stored around the stomach and organs, rather than on the hips and thighs, is what raises the risk of diabetes, high blood pressure and heart disease. It can be high even when BMI looks moderate. Measure around your middle, halfway between the bottom of your ribs and the top of your hips, after breathing out. **[CHECK]**
- Men: 94 cm (37 inches) or more is raised; 102 cm (40 inches) or more is high.
- Women: 80 cm (31.5 inches) or more is raised; 88 cm (34.5 inches) or more is high.

### What is waist-to-height ratio?
Divide your waist measurement by your height, in the same units. A result above 0.5 means your waist is more than half your height, a sign of raised health risk. It is a simple check that works for people of any height. For example, a person 170 cm tall should keep their waist below 85 cm.

### Is BMI accurate for everyone?
No. BMI is a screening tool, not a verdict. A very muscular person can have a high BMI with little fat, and an older person can have a "healthy" BMI while carrying fat around the middle. That is why waist size and your other health measures are looked at together.

### What does not work?
Crash diets, slimming teas, waist trainers and "flat tummy" products do not produce lasting fat loss. Most lead to a rebound, because nothing about daily life has changed.

### What does help?
- A modest, steady reduction in how much you eat, mostly familiar food in better proportions.
- Protein and fibre at each meal, which keep you full.
- Activity on most days, such as a brisk 30-minute walk.
- Enough sleep, and support from someone who checks in regularly.
- Weighing yourself and measuring your waist regularly, so you can see the trend.
Losing 5% to 10% of your weight and keeping it off for a year measurably improves blood pressure, blood sugar and cholesterol. If your BMI is 30 or more, or your weight comes with raised sugar or blood pressure, it deserves structured medical support, which may include medicine for some people.

### Guidelines this article follows
World Health Organization BMI and waist circumference classification, used by the Federal Ministry of Health, Nigeria, and in Tarragon's clinical pathways. NICE guideline NG246: Overweight and obesity management.
Where Nigerian national guidance and NICE differ, this article follows the Nigerian guideline, which is also what Tarragon's care team uses.

## 4. Diabetes medicines explained

**Slug:** `diabetes-medications-explained`
**Title:** Diabetes medicines explained: metformin, gliclazide, insulin and newer tablets
**Description:** How the main type 2 diabetes medicines work, common names used in Nigeria, side effects, low sugar, and questions to ask your doctor.

### Why are there so many diabetes medicines?
Different medicines work on different parts of the problem: some help your body respond better to its own insulin, some help the pancreas release more insulin, and some help the kidneys remove extra sugar in the urine. Many people end up on more than one, not necessarily because they are getting worse, but because diabetes control often needs more than one approach.

### Which medicine is usually started first?
Metformin. It reduces how much sugar the liver releases and helps the body use its own insulin better. It does not cause low blood sugar on its own. Taking it with or after food reduces stomach upset; a slow-release form can help if that persists. Your doctor checks your kidney function before starting it and from time to time after, and may check vitamin B12 if you take it for years. **[CHECK]**

### What is added if metformin is not enough?
Following Nigeria's national diabetes guidelines, the usual second medicine is a sulfonylurea, most often gliclazide, which helps the pancreas release more insulin. Gliclazide is preferred over glibenclamide because it is less likely to cause low sugar. Where they are available and affordable, newer medicines can be used instead or as well:
- DPP-4 inhibitors, such as sitagliptin, which help the body release insulin after meals.
- SGLT2 inhibitors, such as empagliflozin and dapagliflozin, which help the kidneys remove sugar and also protect the heart and kidneys. NICE in the UK recommends these early for people with heart failure, heart disease or a high risk of it.
If two medicines are not enough, a third is added or insulin is started. **[CHECK]**

### When is insulin needed?
People with type 1 diabetes always need insulin. In type 2 diabetes, insulin is started when tablets no longer keep blood sugar in range, during pregnancy, or when someone is very unwell. Starting insulin is not a failure; it is often the most effective way to protect your body. Your care team will show you how to inject, store it and check your sugar.

### What are the common side effects?
- Metformin: stomach upset or loose stools, usually settling within weeks.
- Gliclazide: low blood sugar, and some weight gain.
- SGLT2 inhibitors: more urine, and thrush or other genital infections; keep the area clean and drink enough water.
- Insulin: low blood sugar, and some weight gain.
Tell your doctor about any side effect that bothers you rather than stopping the medicine. There is usually an alternative. **[CHECK]**

### What should I do if my sugar goes too low?
Low blood sugar (below 4.0 mmol/L or 72 mg/dL) can cause shaking, sweating, hunger, a fast heartbeat, confusion or irritability. It is most likely with gliclazide or insulin. Take something sugary straight away, such as glucose tablets, half a glass of a sugary soft drink or juice, or three teaspoons of sugar in water. Check again after 15 minutes and repeat if still low, then eat a snack or your next meal. If someone is drowsy, confused or cannot swallow, do not give food or drink by mouth; get emergency help. **[CHECK against the signed diabetes pathway and EMG wording]**

### What if I am unwell, vomiting or not eating?
When you are ill with vomiting, diarrhoea or a fever and cannot eat or drink normally, some medicines, including metformin and SGLT2 inhibitors, may need to be paused for a day or two, and insulin doses may need adjusting. Ask your care team for your own sick-day plan before you need it, and contact them when you are unwell. **[CHECK]**

### What is worth asking about your own medicine?
- What is this medicine doing, and how will we know it is working?
- Which side effects mean I should contact you rather than wait?
- Can this make my sugar go too low, and what should I do if it does?
- Does it interact with anything else I take, including herbal remedies?
Bring every medicine you take, including herbal ones, to your review.
