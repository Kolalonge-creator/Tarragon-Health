## 1. Cancer screening by age

**Slug:** `cancer-screening-by-age`
**Title:** Cancer screening by age in Nigeria: which tests to have, and when
**Description:** Which cancer screening tests adults need and when: cervical, breast, bowel and prostate, plus warning signs never to wait on.

### What is cancer screening?
Screening means testing people who feel well, to find cancer, or changes that could become cancer, before symptoms start, when treatment is simplest and most successful. It is different from testing someone who already has a symptom: a symptom should be checked straight away, whatever your age.

### Cervical cancer screening: when should women start?
Using the national approach Tarragon follows: **[CHECK]**
- Most women: start at 30 and repeat every 5 to 10 years, depending on the test used.
- Women living with HIV: start at 25 and repeat every 3 to 5 years.
- The preferred test is an HPV DNA test, which finds the virus that causes almost all cervical cancer. Where it is not available, a VIA test (a visual check with vinegar solution) is used.
- Screening can usually stop after 50 once two tests in a row are normal.
HPV vaccination of girls aged 9 to 14 prevents most cervical cancer in the first place.

### Breast cancer screening: what should women do?
- Every woman: know what is normal for your breasts, and report any new lump, skin or nipple change, or discharge without waiting for a screening date.
- A clinical breast examination is part of a health check.
- Mammography (a breast X-ray) is commonly offered from about 40 to 69 in Nigerian programmes, and earlier or more often for women with a strong family history.
**[CHECK]**

### Bowel (colorectal) cancer screening: who needs it?
Screening is offered from about 45 to 50 until 75, using a stool test (FIT) that looks for hidden blood, or a colonoscopy. It may start earlier with a strong family history. **[CHECK]**

### Prostate cancer screening: should men have a PSA test?
The PSA blood test is not a routine test for every man, because it can find cancers that would never have caused harm and lead to tests and treatment that are not needed. It is offered after a conversation about the benefits and drawbacks, usually from about 50 to 69. Men of African ancestry, including Nigerian men, have a higher risk of prostate cancer, so the conversation is worth having from 45, or earlier with a father or brother who had it. **[CHECK]**

### What about other cancers?
- Liver cancer: hepatitis B is a major cause in Nigeria, so a hepatitis B test, and vaccination if you are not immune, is one of the most useful cancer-prevention steps there is.
- Lung, ovarian and other cancers: there is no general screening for people without symptoms; report symptoms early instead.

### Which warning signs should never wait for screening?
See a doctor promptly for: a new lump anywhere; bleeding after the menopause, between periods or after sex; blood in your stool or urine; a change in bowel habit lasting more than three weeks; unexplained weight loss; a cough or hoarseness lasting more than three weeks; or a sore or mole that changes or will not heal.

### Guidelines this article follows
Federal Ministry of Health, Nigeria: National Cancer Control Plan, and the national approach to cervical cancer prevention. World Health Organization guidance on cervical cancer screening and treatment (2021). Tarragon's annual health check pathway. For comparison, UK National Screening Committee programmes and NICE guideline NG131 (prostate cancer) and NG12 (suspected cancer: recognition and referral).
Where Nigerian national guidance and NICE differ, this article follows the Nigerian guideline, which is also what Tarragon's care team uses.

## 2. HIV and hepatitis screening

**Slug:** `hiv-hepatitis-screening-why-test`
**Title:** HIV, hepatitis B and hepatitis C tests: why every adult should test at least once
**Description:** Why every adult in Nigeria should test for HIV, hepatitis B and hepatitis C at least once, what the tests are, and what a positive result means today.

### Why test if I feel well?
HIV, hepatitis B and hepatitis C can each live in the body for years without any symptoms while quietly damaging the immune system or the liver. Many people in Nigeria living with HIV or hepatitis B do not know it. A test is the only way to know, and finding it early makes treatment simpler and protects the people around you.

### Who should get tested?
Every adult, at least once, whether or not you think you are at risk. Test again if: **[CHECK]**
- you are pregnant (HIV, hepatitis B and syphilis tests are part of antenatal care);
- you start a new relationship, or a partner has tested positive;
- you have had unprotected sex, shared needles, or a blood transfusion or procedure with unsterile equipment;
- a family member has hepatitis B;
- you work in healthcare and have had a needle-stick injury.

### What tests are used?
- HIV: a rapid test from a finger prick or a blood test. Results can be ready the same day; a positive rapid test is always confirmed with further tests.
- Hepatitis B: the HBsAg blood test shows whether you currently carry the virus. Other tests show whether you are immune from vaccination or past infection.
- Hepatitis C: an antibody test, followed by a confirmatory test if positive.
No fasting is needed. Results are confidential between you and your care team.

### What happens if my HIV test is positive?
HIV is now a long-term condition that can be managed well. Daily treatment (antiretroviral therapy) keeps the immune system healthy and allows a long, full life. When treatment keeps the virus undetectable in the blood, HIV cannot be passed on to a sexual partner. In Nigeria, HIV treatment is available free through national programmes, and starting it as soon as possible after diagnosis is recommended. **[CHECK]**

### What happens if my hepatitis B test is positive?
Most adults with long-term hepatitis B need regular check-ups of the liver, with blood tests and sometimes a scan, to decide whether medicine is needed. Some people need tablets that control the virus for many years. Close family members and partners should be tested and vaccinated if they are not immune. **[CHECK]**

### Can hepatitis C be cured?
Yes. Modern tablets taken for 8 to 12 weeks cure hepatitis C in most people. That is why testing matters: a cure is available once it is found. **[CHECK]**

### How can I protect myself?
- Hepatitis B vaccination: three doses give long-lasting protection. Ask to be tested first, and vaccinated if you are not immune.
- Use condoms, and never share needles, razors or toothbrushes.
- In pregnancy, testing and treatment greatly reduce the chance of passing HIV or hepatitis B to the baby.

### Guidelines this article follows
Federal Ministry of Health, Nigeria: National Guidelines for HIV Prevention, Treatment and Care, and National Guidelines for the Prevention, Care and Treatment of Viral Hepatitis B and C. Tarragon's annual health check pathway. NICE guidelines NG60: HIV testing, and PH43: Hepatitis B and C testing.
Where Nigerian national guidance and NICE differ, this article follows the Nigerian guideline, which is also what Tarragon's care team uses.

## 3. What high sugar does over time: diabetes complications

**Slug:** `diabetes-complications-eyes-kidneys-nerves-heart`
**Title:** Diabetes complications: eyes, kidneys, nerves, feet and heart, and the checks that prevent them
**Description:** How diabetes affects the eyes, kidneys, nerves, feet and heart, early signs to watch for, and the yearly checks that catch problems early.

### Why do diabetes complications build up silently?
Raised blood sugar slowly damages small blood vessels and nerves over years, usually with no symptoms until the damage is advanced. That is why regular checks matter more than how you feel from day to day. Most complications can be prevented or slowed if they are found early.

### How does diabetes affect the eyes?
Diabetic retinopathy is damage to the back of the eye. It is a leading cause of preventable blindness and causes no symptoms in its early, most treatable stage. A retinal examination, with drops to widen the pupil or a photograph of the back of the eye, should be done at least once a year. A normal eyesight check is not enough. Report sudden blurred vision, floaters or loss of vision straight away. **[CHECK]**

### How does diabetes affect the kidneys?
The kidneys filter blood through tiny vessels that raised sugar and blood pressure can damage. A urine test for protein (the albumin-to-creatinine ratio) and a blood test for kidney function (creatinine and eGFR) find early damage years before symptoms. These are done at least once a year. Some diabetes and blood pressure medicines also protect the kidneys. **[CHECK]**

### How does diabetes affect the nerves and feet?
Nerve damage (neuropathy) often starts as tingling, burning or numbness in the feet. Numb feet may not feel a cut or blister, and poor circulation slows healing, which is how small foot wounds can become serious. Check your feet every day, wear well-fitting shoes, never walk barefoot, and have your feet examined at every review. A foot wound that is red, swollen, smells or is not healing needs a doctor the same day. **[CHECK]**

### How does diabetes affect the heart and blood vessels?
Diabetes roughly doubles the risk of heart attack and stroke. That is why blood pressure and cholesterol are treated alongside blood sugar. For most adults with diabetes, Tarragon's care team aims for a blood pressure below 130/80, and many people with diabetes are offered a statin to protect their heart. NICE in the UK uses a target of below 140/90 for most adults; Tarragon follows the Nigerian guideline. **[CHECK]**

### Are there other complications?
Diabetes can also cause erectile difficulties, gum disease, slower healing of skin, and more frequent infections, including urine and genital infections. These are common and treatable; mention them at your review.

### Which checks should I have every year?
- HbA1c every 3 to 6 months, and blood pressure at every review.
- Retinal (eye) examination: at least once a year.
- Urine protein (ACR) and kidney blood test: at least once a year.
- Foot examination: at every review, with a full check at least once a year.
- Cholesterol: once a year.
**[CHECK against the signed diabetes pathway]**

### What slows or prevents complications?
Keeping blood sugar in your target range over time (most adults aim for an HbA1c below 7%), controlling blood pressure and cholesterol, not smoking, staying active, and keeping up with the yearly checks. None of this is exotic; it is the evidence behind why the daily routine matters as much as it does.

### Guidelines this article follows
Federal Ministry of Health, Nigeria: National Guidelines on the Prevention, Control and Management of Diabetes Mellitus. Diabetes Association of Nigeria: National Clinical Practice Guidelines for Diabetes Management in Nigeria. Tarragon's diabetes and hypertension pathways. NICE guidelines NG28: Type 2 diabetes in adults, and NG19: Diabetic foot problems.
Where Nigerian national guidance and NICE differ, this article follows the Nigerian guideline, which is also what Tarragon's care team uses.

## 4. Statins: what they actually do, and the myths that stop people taking them

**Slug:** `statins-myths-explained`
**Title:** Statins: what they do, side effects, and the myths that stop people taking them
**Description:** How statins protect the heart, who is offered one, the real side effects, and the common myths about liver damage and muscle pain.

### What does a statin do?
A statin lowers LDL cholesterol by slowing how much cholesterol your liver makes. It also helps stabilise the fatty build-up already in artery walls, making it less likely to break off and cause a heart attack or stroke. It is one of the most studied medicines in the world. Atorvastatin is the statin most often used.

### Who is offered a statin?
A statin is offered based on your overall risk of heart attack and stroke, not on cholesterol alone. People commonly offered one include those who have had a heart attack, stroke or narrowed arteries; most adults with diabetes, especially over 40; people with chronic kidney disease; and people whose estimated 10-year heart and stroke risk is high. Your doctor will explain your own risk. **[CHECK]**

### Myth: "Statins damage your liver"
Serious liver injury from statins is rare. Your doctor checks liver function with a blood test before you start, and again within the first year, as a precaution. A small rise in liver tests is common and usually harmless. Most people never need to stop for it. **[CHECK]**

### Myth: "Statins always cause muscle pain"
Muscle aches are the most common complaint, and they are real for some people. But studies comparing statins with dummy tablets found most of the muscle pain happened on the dummy tablets too. If aches do appear, tell your doctor rather than stopping: changing the dose or switching to a different statin usually solves it. Severe muscle pain with weakness or dark urine is rare but needs a doctor straight away. **[CHECK]**

### Myth: "Once my cholesterol is normal, I can stop"
A normal cholesterol result on a statin usually means the statin is working. Stopping it lets cholesterol and risk rise again. Statins are usually long-term treatment.

### Do statins cause diabetes?
Statins can raise blood sugar slightly, and a small number of people who were already close to diabetes develop it sooner. For people who are offered a statin, the protection against heart attack and stroke is much larger than this risk. **[CHECK]**

### What else should I know?
- Take it at the same time each day; some statins work best in the evening, your pharmacist will advise.
- Avoid large amounts of grapefruit juice with some statins.
- Do not take a statin if you are pregnant or breastfeeding; tell your doctor if you are planning a pregnancy.
- Tell your doctor about all other medicines and herbal remedies, as a few interact with statins.
**[CHECK]**

### What if side effects are bothering me?
Call your doctor rather than stopping quietly. There is almost always another option: a lower dose, a different statin, taking it every other day, or another type of cholesterol medicine. Stopping without a plan removes one of the best-proven protections against heart attack and stroke.

### Guidelines this article follows
NICE guideline NG238: Cardiovascular disease, risk assessment and reduction, including lipid modification. Tarragon's clinical pathways, based on WHO cardiovascular risk guidance. Federal Ministry of Health, Nigeria: National Guidelines on the Prevention, Control and Management of Diabetes Mellitus (statins in diabetes); there is no separate Nigerian national guideline on statins.
Where Nigerian national guidance and NICE differ, this article follows the Nigerian guideline, which is also what Tarragon's care team uses.
