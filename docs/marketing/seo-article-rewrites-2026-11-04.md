## 1. HbA1c: the 3-month sugar test, explained simply

**Slug:** `hba1c-explained` (unchanged, so existing links and Google's record keep working)
**Title:** HbA1c: the 3-month sugar test, explained simply
**Description (shown under the title on Google, max ~155 characters):**
What the 3-month sugar test (HbA1c) is, what your result means, whether you need to fast, and how often to test.

### What is the 3-month sugar test called?
The test many people call the "3-month sugar test" or "3 months average sugar test" is HbA1c. On a laboratory form you may also see it written as A1C, glycated haemoglobin or glycosylated haemoglobin. They are all the same test.
It shows your average blood sugar over the past two to three months, rather than your sugar at the moment the blood is taken.

### How does it measure three months of sugar?
Sugar in your blood sticks to haemoglobin, the protein inside red blood cells. Red blood cells live for about three months, so the share of haemoglobin with sugar attached reflects your average sugar over that time.
That is why one fasting sugar can look normal on a good morning while HbA1c tells the fuller story.

### Do I need to fast before the test?
No. HbA1c does not need fasting and can be done at any time of day. Some laboratories take a fasting sugar from the same sample, so check whether your request also includes a fasting test.

### What is a normal HbA1c?
Results are given as a percentage, and many laboratories also give the same result in mmol/mol. As a widely used guide for adults: **[CHECK thresholds and the mmol/mol equivalents]**
- below 5.7% (below 39 mmol/mol): in the normal range;
- 5.7% to 6.4% (39 to 47 mmol/mol): prediabetes, a warning zone where changes now can prevent diabetes;
- 6.5% or higher (48 mmol/mol or higher): supports a diagnosis of diabetes when confirmed on a laboratory test, usually with a second test.
If you already live with diabetes, your doctor sets a personal target, often around 7% (53 mmol/mol). They balance good control against the risk of sugar going too low, so your target is agreed with you rather than a single number for everyone.

### Can anything make an HbA1c result wrong?
Yes. HbA1c relies on red blood cells and haemoglobin, so anything that changes them can make the number misleading. **[CHECK]**
- haemoglobin variants, which are common in Nigeria, such as sickle cell trait (AS), sickle cell disease (SS) or haemoglobin C. Some laboratory methods are affected by these and some are not, so tell the laboratory and your doctor your genotype if you know it;
- anaemia, recent heavy bleeding or a recent blood transfusion;
- pregnancy;
- advanced kidney disease.
In these situations your doctor may rely more on fasting sugar or other tests.

### How often should I test?
- If you have diabetes: usually every three to six months, to see whether your plan is working.
- If your result was in the prediabetes range: usually once a year.
- If you have risk factors such as a parent with diabetes, excess weight around the waist, high blood pressure, or a past high sugar reading: ask about testing rather than waiting for symptoms. Raised sugar often causes no symptoms for years.
**[CHECK intervals against the signed diabetes pathway]**

### Where can I do an HbA1c test in Nigeria?
Most laboratories in Nigerian cities offer HbA1c. You need a small blood sample from a vein or, at some laboratories, a finger prick. On Tarragon, your care team can write the request for you, you take it to a laboratory you choose and pay them directly, and you upload the result so it is kept on your record and followed up.

### What to do with your number
HbA1c is not a judgement of you; it is feedback. Food, movement, sleep, stress and medicines all move it, and it responds within a few months. People bring their HbA1c down every day, usually with small steady changes and the right medicine, reviewed regularly.

## 2. Blood pressure medicines: the main types, their names, and what to ask

**Slug:** `blood-pressure-medications-explained`
**Title:** Blood pressure medicines: the main types and names, in plain language
**Description:** The main types of BP medicine, common names you will see in Nigeria, usual side effects, and questions worth asking your doctor.

### Why are there so many blood pressure medicines?
Blood pressure is controlled by several systems in the body: how much fluid you carry, how tightly your blood vessels squeeze and how hard your heart pumps. Each type of medicine works on a different part of this. That is why many people end up on two medicines, often at lower doses each, which together control pressure better than one at a high dose.

### What are the main types of BP medicine?
Each type below lists generic names commonly used in Nigeria. Brand names vary by pharmacy, so the generic name is the one to check on the pack. **[CHECK class examples and side effects]**

**Calcium channel blockers** relax the walls of blood vessels.
Common example: amlodipine, nifedipine (slow-release).
Possible side effects: ankle swelling, flushing, headache.

**ACE inhibitors** relax blood vessels by blocking a hormone that narrows them, and help protect the kidneys, especially with diabetes.
Common examples: lisinopril, enalapril, ramipril.
Possible side effect: a dry cough. Not for use in pregnancy.

**ARBs (angiotensin receptor blockers)** work like ACE inhibitors and are often used when an ACE inhibitor causes a cough.
Common examples: losartan, valsartan, telmisartan.
Not for use in pregnancy.

**Diuretics ("water tablets")** help the body release extra salt and water.
Common examples: hydrochlorothiazide, indapamide, chlorthalidone.
Possible side effects: passing more urine, changes in salt levels in the blood (checked with blood tests).

**Beta blockers** slow the heart. They are used more selectively today, often when there is a specific heart condition as well.
Common examples: atenolol, bisoprolol, carvedilol.

Many people take a combination tablet that contains two of these in one pill, which makes daily treatment simpler.

### Which BP medicine is usually started first?
It depends on your age, other conditions such as diabetes or kidney disease, pregnancy plans and how your body responds. International guidelines often suggest a calcium channel blocker or a diuretic as a first choice for adults of Black African descent, and an ACE inhibitor or ARB when diabetes or kidney disease is also present. **[CHECK wording against the signed hypertension pathway]** Your doctor may choose differently for good reasons, so ask what the reason is.

### How long before the medicine works?
Most BP medicines start to lower pressure within days, and the full effect usually shows after two to four weeks. That is why your doctor may wait a few weeks before changing the dose. Keep checking your blood pressure at home in the meantime so the decision is based on real readings.

### Can I stop my BP medicine when I feel fine?
No, not without talking to your doctor first. High blood pressure usually causes no symptoms, so feeling well does not mean it is controlled; the medicine is often the reason the readings are good. If cost, side effects or getting your refill is the real problem, say so. There is almost always an equally effective option.

### What is worth asking about your own medicine?
- What is this medicine doing, and when should my readings start to change?
- Which side effects mean I should contact you rather than wait?
- Should I take it in the morning or the evening?
- What should I do if I miss a dose?

## 3. Why crash diets don't work, and what keeps weight off

**Slug:** `why-crash-diets-dont-work`
**Title:** Why crash diets don't work, and what actually keeps weight off
**Description:** Why crash diets fail and the weight comes back, what counts as a crash diet, and the steady approach that keeps weight off.

### Why isn't a crash diet a healthy solution?
A crash diet cuts food so sharply that the body treats it as a famine. Weight falls quickly at first, but much of that early loss is water and some is muscle rather than fat. When normal eating returns, the weight usually comes back, often with a little more. That is biology, not a lack of willpower.

### What counts as a crash diet?
Signs that a plan is a crash diet: **[CHECK]**
- eating far less than your body needs, for example living on one small meal a day or only liquids;
- cutting out whole food groups with no plan for what replaces them;
- promising more than about 1 kg of weight loss a week for weeks on end;
- relying on "detox" teas, laxatives or slimming pills.

### What happens in the body during a crash diet?
- The body lowers its energy use to save fuel, so the same food later goes further than before.
- Hunger hormones rise and the "full" signal weakens, which makes overeating more likely when the diet ends.
- Muscle is lost alongside fat, and less muscle means the body burns less energy at rest.
- Some people feel dizzy or tired, find it hard to concentrate, or get constipated. For people taking medicine for diabetes or blood pressure, a sudden large drop in food can make sugar or pressure fall too low.

### What keeps weight off instead?
- **A modest daily reduction**, not a starvation plan. Losing about 0.5 to 1 kg a week is more likely to last. **[CHECK]**
- **Familiar food in better proportions:** more vegetables and beans, a smaller portion of swallow or rice, grilled rather than fried protein, and fewer sugary drinks. You do not need imported "diet" foods.
- **Regular movement** you can keep up, such as a daily brisk walk. It helps keep muscle while weight comes down.
- **Sleep and stress:** short sleep raises hunger the next day.
- **Support:** checking in with someone regularly makes a plan far more likely to last.

### Track the trend, not the day
Weight moves up and down by a kilogram or more from day to day because of water and salt. Weigh yourself at the same time of day, a few times a week, and look at the trend over a month. That trend, not one morning's number, tells you whether the plan is working.

### Who should talk to a doctor before changing their diet?
Talk to a doctor first if you take medicine for diabetes or blood pressure, are pregnant or breastfeeding, have kidney disease, or have had an eating disorder. Your medicines may need adjusting as your weight and eating change.

## 4. How to measure your blood pressure at home, properly

**Slug:** `home-blood-pressure-monitoring`
**Title:** How to check your blood pressure at home, properly
**Description:** A step-by-step routine for accurate home blood pressure readings: rest five minutes, take two readings a minute apart, and record them.

### Why check your blood pressure at home?
Readings taken at home, when you are relaxed, are often more accurate than one reading at a clinic, where many people's pressure rises from nerves or rushing. A week of home readings gives your doctor a much clearer picture than a single visit.

### What kind of monitor should I use?
Use an automatic upper-arm monitor rather than a wrist or finger device; arm monitors are generally more accurate. **[CHECK]** The cuff size matters: a cuff that is too small gives falsely high readings, so measure around the middle of your upper arm and match it to the size printed on the cuff. Any brand sold by a reputable pharmacy is fine; if you can, choose one listed as clinically validated.

### Step by step: how to take an accurate reading **[CHECK]**
1. For 30 minutes before, do not exercise, smoke, or drink coffee, tea or other caffeinated drinks.
2. Empty your bladder first.
3. Sit in a chair with your back supported and both feet flat on the floor. Do not cross your legs.
4. Rest quietly for 5 minutes. No phone, no talking.
5. Rest your arm on a table so the cuff is level with your heart. Put the cuff on bare skin, not over clothing.
6. Take a reading, wait 1 minute, and take a second reading.
7. Record both readings with the date and time, or log them in the app.

### When and how often should I check?
When your doctor is assessing your blood pressure or changing your medicine, a common routine is two readings each morning before medicine and two each evening, for seven days. After that, follow the routine your doctor gives you. **[CHECK against the signed hypertension pathway]**

### Five mistakes that push a reading up
- a cuff that is too small;
- talking during the reading;
- an unsupported arm, or an arm below heart level;
- sitting with crossed legs or an unsupported back;
- measuring straight after climbing stairs, a meal or coffee.

### What is a normal home reading?
Home readings are usually a little lower than clinic readings. As a widely used guide, an average home reading of 135/85 or higher suggests high blood pressure. Your doctor will tell you your own target. **[CHECK threshold]**

### When is a reading an emergency?
If a reading is very high, for example 180/120 or above, rest for 5 minutes and check again. If it is still that high, or if at any reading you have chest pain, shortness of breath, weakness or numbness on one side, difficulty speaking, sudden severe headache or blurred vision, go to the nearest hospital now. **[CHECK: must match the signed emergency wording (EMG/TRI) and the app's red-flag thresholds]**

### What to do with your readings
Bring them to every review, or log them in Tarragon, where each reading is checked against care protocols and your care team sees the trend. One high reading is rarely a crisis; a pattern over a week is what guides treatment.
