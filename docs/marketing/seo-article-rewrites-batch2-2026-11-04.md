## 1. Why you shouldn't stop your blood pressure medicine when you feel fine

**Slug:** `blood-pressure-medication-myths`
**Title:** Can I stop my blood pressure medicine when I feel fine? Common myths, answered
**Description:** Why feeling well doesn't mean blood pressure is controlled, the common myths about BP medicine, and what to do instead of stopping.

### Can I stop my blood pressure medicine if I feel fine?
Not without talking to your doctor first. High blood pressure usually causes no symptoms at all, so a person whose pressure is well controlled by medicine feels exactly the same as one whose pressure is quietly climbing again. Feeling fine is not a sign that you no longer need the medicine; very often it is a sign that the medicine is working. Only a blood pressure reading can tell you.

### What happens if I stop suddenly?
For most blood pressure medicines, pressure drifts back towards its untreated level within days to a couple of weeks, with no warning sign. The risk of stroke and heart attack rises with it. A few medicines, beta blockers in particular, should never be stopped suddenly, because the heart can react with a fast pulse or chest pain; they need to be reduced gradually with a doctor's advice. **[CHECK]**

### Myth: "BP medicine damages the kidneys"
Uncontrolled high blood pressure is one of the commonest causes of kidney damage. Most blood pressure medicines protect the kidneys by lowering the pressure on them, and some (ACE inhibitors and ARBs) are chosen specifically for that. Your doctor checks kidney function with a blood test from time to time, especially when starting or changing some medicines, so that any rare problem is picked up early. **[CHECK]**

### Myth: "Once my blood pressure is normal, I am cured"
For most people, high blood pressure is a long-term condition that is controlled, not cured. A normal reading on medicine usually means the treatment is working. Some people can reduce or stop a medicine after real, lasting changes such as significant weight loss, much less salt and regular exercise, but only with their doctor watching the readings as the dose comes down.

### Myth: "Herbs or bitter leaf can replace my tablets"
Some foods and lifestyle changes help lower blood pressure: less salt, more vegetables and fruit, regular activity, less alcohol, not smoking. Herbal mixtures are a different matter. Their strength varies from bottle to bottle, they have not been tested to show they prevent strokes or heart attacks, and some interact with prescribed medicines. If you use or want to try a herbal remedy, tell your doctor rather than swapping it for your tablets. **[CHECK]**

### Myth: "If I take it every day my body will get used to it"
Blood pressure medicines do not stop working because you take them regularly, and they are not addictive. If readings rise again over time, it is usually because blood pressure tends to rise with age or weight, not because the body has "got used to" the medicine. The answer is a review of the dose or a second medicine.

### Why do people stop?
The reasons are rarely medical: running out and delaying a refill, a busy week that breaks the routine, a rumour about side effects, cost, or simply feeling well. Each of these has a better answer than stopping.

### If cost, side effects or refills are the problem
Tell your doctor the real reason. There is almost always an equally effective alternative: a cheaper generic version, a once-a-day tablet instead of twice, a combination pill that replaces two, or a different medicine if one causes side effects. A plan that fits your life is one you can keep.

## 2. Hypertension complications: what high blood pressure does to your heart, brain, kidneys and eyes

**Slug:** `hypertension-complications-heart-brain-kidneys`
**Title:** Complications of high blood pressure: heart, brain, kidneys and eyes
**Description:** Why high blood pressure is called a silent killer, what it does to the heart, brain, kidneys and eyes, and how treatment lowers each risk.

### Why is high blood pressure called a silent killer?
High blood pressure usually causes no symptoms. Meanwhile, the constant extra force on the walls of the arteries slowly damages blood vessels, including those supplying the heart, brain, kidneys and eyes. The first sign is often a complication such as a stroke rather than the blood pressure itself. That is why regular checks matter even when you feel well.

### What does high blood pressure do to the heart?
- **Heart attack:** raised pressure speeds up the narrowing of the arteries that supply the heart.
- **Heart failure:** pumping against high pressure for years makes the heart muscle thicken and, eventually, weaken, so it cannot pump as well. Signs include breathlessness, swollen ankles and tiredness.
- **Irregular heartbeat:** such as atrial fibrillation, which itself raises the risk of stroke.
**[CHECK]**

### What does high blood pressure do to the brain?
High blood pressure is the biggest risk factor you can change for stroke, both the kind caused by a blocked artery and the kind caused by bleeding in the brain. Over many years it is also linked to small, silent changes in the brain that can affect memory and thinking later in life. Controlling blood pressure is one of the most effective ways to prevent stroke.

**Know the signs of a stroke, and act fast:** sudden weakness or drooping of one side of the face, weakness of an arm or leg, slurred or confused speech, sudden loss of vision, or a sudden severe headache. If you notice any of these in yourself or someone else, go to the nearest hospital immediately; every minute counts. **[CHECK: must match the signed emergency wording]**

### What does high blood pressure do to the kidneys?
The kidneys filter blood through very small, delicate blood vessels, and raised pressure damages them over time. Kidney damage then raises blood pressure further, so each makes the other worse. Kidney disease usually causes no symptoms until it is advanced, which is why people with high blood pressure are offered a urine test and a blood test (creatinine) to check their kidneys. **[CHECK]**

### What does high blood pressure do to the eyes?
The small blood vessels at the back of the eye can be damaged too (hypertensive retinopathy). Mild changes cause no symptoms and are found during an eye examination. Very high blood pressure can cause sudden blurred vision, which needs urgent attention.

### What about pregnancy?
High blood pressure in pregnancy, including pre-eclampsia, needs close monitoring because it affects both mother and baby. Anyone with high blood pressure who is pregnant or planning to be should talk to their doctor early, as some blood pressure medicines are not used in pregnancy. **[CHECK]**

### Which complications can treatment prevent?
Every one of these risks falls substantially once blood pressure is brought under control and kept there. Lowering a raised blood pressure reduces the risk of stroke and heart attack within a few years of treatment. The benefit depends on staying on treatment, which is why ongoing monitoring and taking medicine consistently matter more than any single reading.

### Who is most at risk?
The risk of complications is higher when blood pressure has been high for a long time or is very high, and when it occurs together with diabetes, smoking, high cholesterol, kidney disease or excess weight. Each of these can be checked and improved, which is why a care plan looks at all of them together.

## 4. Diabetes and your plate: what to eat in a Nigerian kitchen

**Slug:** `diabetes-diet-nigerian-kitchen`
**Title:** What to eat with diabetes in Nigeria: a practical guide for a Nigerian kitchen
**Description:** What to eat with diabetes using everyday Nigerian food: portions, better swaps for swallow, rice and plantain, and drinks to limit.

### Do I have to stop eating swallow, rice or yam?
No food is banned outright. Eba, pounded yam, amala, semo, rice and other staples can stay on your table. What matters most is how much you eat, what you eat it with, and how often. A smaller portion of swallow with plenty of vegetable soup and protein raises blood sugar far less than a large portion eaten with little else.

### How big should my portion of swallow or rice be?
A simple guide is the plate method: fill half the plate with vegetables (or a generous vegetable soup), a quarter with protein such as fish, chicken, beans, eggs or lean meat, and a quarter with the starchy food. For swallow, many people find a portion about the size of their fist is a good starting point. Your care team can help you adjust this to your own readings. **[CHECK]**

### Which starchy foods are better choices?
Foods with more fibre and less processing raise blood sugar more slowly:
- unripe or less ripe plantain rather than very ripe plantain or dodo;
- beans, moi moi and other legumes, which are both starch and protein;
- brown rice, or a smaller portion of white rice mixed with beans (rice and beans);
- whole-grain or unrefined options where available, such as ofada rice or wheat swallow, in sensible portions;
- boiled or roasted yam and potatoes rather than fried.
**[CHECK]**

### Which vegetables and soups help?
Leafy vegetables and soups such as ugu, waterleaf, bitter leaf, okra, efo riro and vegetable soup add fibre and fullness with little effect on blood sugar. Go easy on the palm oil and seasoning cubes, which add fat and salt, and make the vegetables the main part of the soup rather than a garnish.

### Does the order I eat in matter?
Yes. Eating the vegetables and protein before the starchy part of the meal can noticeably reduce the rise in blood sugar afterwards. In practice: start with the soup's vegetables and protein, and have the swallow or rice towards the end of the meal.

### What should I drink?
Water is best. Drinks are where the most sugar slips in unnoticed:
- soft drinks, malt drinks and sweetened juices raise blood sugar quickly; keep them for rare occasions, if at all;
- zobo and kunu are often heavily sweetened; ask for or make them without added sugar;
- if you take sugar in tea or pap, reduce it gradually; most people stop noticing within a few weeks.
Alcohol can make blood sugar fall too low, especially if you take insulin or certain tablets, so talk to your doctor about what is safe for you. **[CHECK]**

### What about fruit?
Fruit is fine in sensible portions: a piece of orange, a slice of pawpaw or a small handful of grapes, eaten whole rather than as juice. Very sweet fruits such as ripe mango and pineapple are best in smaller portions, ideally with or after a meal.

### What is worth cutting back?
Deep-fried foods and packaged snacks such as chin chin, puff puff and biscuits add sugar and the kind of fat that works against your numbers over time. Small, consistent changes you can keep up, week after week, do more than a dramatic change that lasts a fortnight.

### How do I know if my diet is working?
Your blood sugar readings and your HbA1c (the 3-month sugar test) tell you. If you check your sugar at home, a reading about two hours after a meal shows how that meal affected you. Bring your readings to your review, or log them in Tarragon, and your care team will look at the pattern with you.
