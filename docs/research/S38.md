# S38 research: outcome snapshots and de-identified analytics

Date: 2026-10-06. Public sources only. "Unverified" means I did not find a public document confirming it; treat as inference or industry folklore.
Scope note: H2O.ai and Ada Health publish no outcome-reporting or cohort-suppression mechanics that I found; Medisafe publishes no adherence-metric definition I could find. Claims about them are unverified and omitted.

## 22.1 Baseline and follow-up outcomes (BP control at 90 and 180 days)

- Standard definition (WHO HEARTS family, Resolve to Save Lives job aid): control rate = patients with BP under 140/90 at their last visit in the window, divided by ALL patients enrolled. People lost to follow-up or deceased stay in the denominator and count as not controlled. Missed-visit rate and lost-to-follow-up are reported alongside, never instead.
- Cohort variant (HEARTS studies): enrol on baseline BP of 140/90 or above, then report the share controlled at a fixed follow-up. The exact "registered at 3 months" cohort wording in the job aid was not fully confirmed (unverified).
- US NQF 0018 / Million Hearts: most recent BP in the year, both numbers under 140/90, clinic-recorded readings only; exclusions are ESRD, dialysis, transplant, CKD stage 5, pregnancy, hospice. Home readings are not accepted there, so Tarragon must declare its own source rule.
- Competitors: Omada's published hypertension study reports mean systolic change at 12 months in a retrospective cohort, split by uncontrolled baseline. Lark reports mean systolic drop at 3 months among those who reached month 3 (survivor-filtered). Both are mean change, not control rate.
- Tarragon should adapt: snapshots at day 0/30/90/180 store the reading used, its source (clinic, device, self-entered), and a window (for example day 90 plus or minus 14). Headline = strict denominator (everyone enrolled with baseline) with no reading counted as not controlled; show secondary "among those measured". Always show both plus the missing share. Exclude pregnancy and flagged exclusions explicitly with counts.
- Sources: resolvetosavelives.org hypertension indicators job aid and FAQs; JACC Intl 2023 Global Hearts (doi 10.1016/j.jacc.2023.08.043); qpp.cms.gov HTN-2 NQF 0018 spec; JMIR Cardio 2023 (doi 10.2196/43489); JMIR Form Res 2022 (doi 10.2196/38215).

## 22.2 Adherence and engagement, kept separate from clinical outcomes

- Standard: medication adherence is usually proportion of days covered (days with supply divided by days in period), with 80 percent as the common cutoff (HEDIS style). Engagement (logins, readings logged) is a different construct.
- Lark's paper links engagement to BP change but is observational; engagement predicting outcome is not the same as engagement being an outcome.
- Tarragon should adapt: three separate panels (clinical, adherence, engagement) with no composite score. Adherence uses refill or self-report coverage labelled by source; engagement counts readings per week and care team contacts. Never show engagement as evidence that BP improved.
- Sources: HEDIS PDC description (mclaren.org tip sheet); PDC estimation paper, PMC8715592; JMIR Form Res 2022.

## 22.3 Risk stratification for clinician worklists

- Innovaccer and Health Catalyst market red/yellow/green risk tiers feeding care-gap lists (Health Catalyst cites the Johns Hopkins ACG method). Their data-quality rule counts were vendor claims (Innovaccer says 6000 rules), unverified independently.
- Obermeyer 2019 (Science): a widely used algorithm used cost as a proxy for need and under-selected sicker Black patients for extra care; fix was changing the label. Lesson for a Nigerian platform: never train on spend or visit counts, since poorer patients generate less of both.
- Tarragon should adapt: deterministic, explainable rules (latest BP band, missed readings, symptoms, medicines gap) that only reorder outreach. Show the reason list. A clinician can override and the override is stored. Low-risk never means no contact.
- Sources: science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aax2342; innovaccer.com risk stratification blog; healthcatalyst.com population health pages.

## 22.5 Personal monthly progress report

- Competitors (Omada, Lark) show the member their own trend and weight or BP change; their mean-change reporting is cohort level and not appropriate as a personal promise (inference).
- Tarragon should adapt: patient sees only their own readings, the target (under 140/90 unless your care team set another), a plain trend, and what your care team did. Say "not enough readings this month" when data is thin instead of a trend. No comparison to other patients, no ranking, no fear wording.
- Source: Omada and Lark published studies above; Tarragon voice rules.

## 22.6 Aggregate sponsor dashboards, minimum cohort sizes, no causal claims

- CMS cell suppression: no cell with 1 to 10 patients may be shown, and percentages must not reveal such a cell. This is the usual published floor (cohort under 11 suppressed). Complementary suppression is needed so subtraction from totals does not reveal a hidden cell.
- NDPA 2023 and the NDPC GAID 2025 (effective 19 Sept 2025): require privacy by design and consideration of anonymisation or pseudonymisation. I found no published numeric small-cell threshold from NDPC (unverified); adopt 11 as policy and say so.
- Tarragon should adapt: sponsor views are aggregate only, cohort of at least 11 (consider 20 for any cut by two attributes), suppress and merge categories below it, round or band small counts, no individual rows, no free text, no exact dates (use month). Label as descriptive, reported "alongside" enrolment, never "caused by".
- Sources: cms.gov data use agreement and cell suppression policy (hhs.gov guidance document); ndpc.gov.ng GAID 2025 PDF.

## 22.7 Escalation and clinician response-time metrics

- Public precedent for per-person audit exists in MTS triage research (see S20); no competitor publishes response-time league tables (unverified).
- Tarragon should adapt: measure time from alert to first care team action against the active `escalation_slas` row, as median and share within SLA, per queue and not per named clinician in anything shared externally. Include time to patient contact and unresolved count. Show denominator and out-of-hours split.
- Source: S20 note; internal SLA table.

## 22.8 Data-quality indicators and missing-data reporting

- Nigeria evidence: facility-reported data has been found incomplete in up to about 40 percent of reports and events under-recorded in registers (WHO-style assessments, via Nigeria health ICT literature); no national patient ID exists, and phone-based offline matching has been proposed because of it.
- Tarragon should adapt: report per snapshot, completeness of baseline BP, share with a day-90 reading, share with any reading, duplicate-suspect rate (shared phone numbers), reading source mix, and lost-to-follow-up share. Print missing counts next to every outcome rate. Block a sponsor report if completeness falls under a set floor and show why.
- Sources: PMC9489848 (offline patient ID, Nigeria); WHO Nigeria health ICT framework PDF.

## 22.9 Exportable pilot and renewal reports

- Competitors summarise results for payers as mean change plus engagement; renewal evidence is typically selective (unverified).
- Tarragon should adapt: fixed template: method paragraph, definition used, denominator flow (enrolled, baseline recorded, day-90 reading, lost to follow-up), strict and measured-only control rate, completeness table, suppressed cells marked "fewer than 11". Version the report and store the query and date. Export carries no patient identifiers and test accounts are excluded by view.
- Source: sections above.

## Unsafe or unsuitable for Nigeria

- Re-identification in small cohorts: a small employer, one clinic or one LGA cut can name a patient. Enforce the minimum cohort and complementary suppression; do not allow filter stacking that shrinks below it.
- Survivorship bias from dropouts: reporting only patients who reached day 90 inflates control. Strict denominator and lost-to-follow-up are mandatory; low smartphone and data access make dropout non-random.
- Using risk scores to deny care: scores order outreach only. Cost or utilisation as a label repeats the Obermeyer failure.
- Causal claims from uncontrolled before/after data: regression to the mean and enrolment at a high BP peak flatter any programme. Use "observed change", never "reduced by".
- Public ranking of clinicians: small caseloads give noisy rates, punishes those taking hard cases and teaches case selection. Keep response-time data internal and aggregate.
- Shared phones and no unique ID: one number may cover a family, so duplicate detection and per-person snapshots need care; do not merge records on phone alone.
- Test accounts must be excluded by the view itself, not by analyst convention.
