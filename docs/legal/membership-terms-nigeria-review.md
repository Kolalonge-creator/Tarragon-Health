# Membership terms: Nigerian law review (research draft)

Prepared 2026-10-13 for the owner (CEO and CMO), who signs the terms personally as the responsible officer. This is research by an AI assistant. It is not legal advice, it is not a legal certification, and no lawyer has reviewed it. Its job is to show what rule each clause is meant to meet, where the rule was actually read, and what a Nigerian lawyer must settle before publication. Draft text under review: `schedule-c-terms-of-service.md` (version 2026-10-13-v1-draft) and the Free plan wording added to `schedule-b-telehealth-consent.md`.

## 3. Items for counsel's attention

**A. Fixed 21-day window and nothing after it.** This is the central legal question. Three FCCPA provisions pull against a clean "no refund after day 21" rule, as read in the reproductions:
- s.120 lets a consumer cancel an advance order or booking and limits the supplier to a *reasonable* cancellation fee, judged by notice given, ability to resell, and industry practice. A fee of 100 percent on day 22 for a service mostly still to come is arguable as unreasonable. A health Membership cannot be resold to someone else, which helps the business, but most of the monthly follow-ups would still be undelivered at month 2.
- s.127 forbids terms that are harsh to the point of being inequitable; the Templars article treats an indiscriminate no-refund policy as such a term.
- s.129(1)(b) voids a term that removes a right under the Act.
Counsel should say whether the 21-day rule survives for *change of mind*, and whether a sliding refund (for example, pro-rata by months left, less a reasonable fee) is needed. The draft protects against the worst reading by never applying the 21-day cut-off to non-delivery or to anything the law requires.

**B. Cooling-off for distance and online contracts.** I found no FCCPA section or FCCPC instrument creating a statutory cooling-off period for online services, but I could not read the official Act and cannot say none exists. Ask counsel whether any FCCPC regulation or guidance for online selling applies, and whether 21 days (longer than the 7 to 14 days common elsewhere) is enough comfort.

**C. What must be refunded if Tarragon fails to deliver the annual test or the calls.** s.130(2) gives the consumer a choice to require the defect fixed or a reasonable portion of the price refunded in proportion to the failure. The draft offers both, at the Member's choice, because the safer reading is that the choice belongs to the consumer. Counsel to confirm: (1) that the choice is the consumer's, not ours; (2) how to value each part (annual blood test, point-of-care checks, about twelve follow-ups) so the pro-rata share is not arbitrary; (3) what "reasonable time" means for the annual test (s.143 summary), including whether a testing-day schedule that offers the Member nothing nearby is a failure on our side; (4) whether a monthly call missed once is a failure or only the third miss is.

**D. Non-excludable liability for negligence and any cap.** s.129(1)(c) voids a cap on liability for gross negligence, and s.144(2) forbids excluding the implied terms of care, skill and reasonable time for a service. The draft therefore has no financial cap and excludes nothing for death, injury or negligence. Counsel to say whether any cap on other loss (for example a limit to fees paid) is safe for a consumer health service, and whether the wording "to the extent the law allows" is acceptable or should be removed. Note s.137 is about goods, not services, so it is not relied on here.

**E. Immediate end of access on refund.** The draft says approved refund ends Member access at once, with no grace. Check that this is not an unfair term when a refund is a statutory remedy for our failure: a Member who takes the make-good-or-refund option because we failed to deliver may reasonably expect continuity of care while we resolve it. The draft only ends access on a refund the Member chose. Counsel to confirm that is fair, and that a clinician must still hand over safely any open prescription or follow-up (a clinical, not legal, question for the CMO).

**F. Health data: lawful basis, retention, and withdrawal.** The terms lean on Schedule A. Three points: (1) NDPA s.30(1)(g) allows sensitive-data processing for medical care by a professional owing confidentiality, so the lawful basis for treatment records may be s.30(1)(g) rather than consent alone; this matters because the draft says withdrawal is always possible and records are kept afterwards. Counsel to reconcile "you can withdraw consent" with "we keep your records" so the document is not misleading (s.26(4), s.127(2)(c)). (2) Schedule A says the retention schedule is "being finalised with counsel"; NDPA s.27(1)(e) requires telling the person the retention period at collection and the GAID asks for a published retention policy. (3) GAID Art. 28 makes a DPIA mandatory for health care services: confirm the existing DPIA was filed with the NDPC.

**G. Telemedicine rules and the Free plan.** I found no telemedicine statute; sources point to the MDCN Code of Medical Ethics 2008 and the National Health Act. Counsel and the CMO should confirm that (1) a Free plan with no doctor alert for red-flag readings is acceptable under the duty of care and the MDCN Code, given that it is a business-model decision and not a clinical one; (2) the statement shown to the Free user is plain enough to count as informed notice; (3) the platform entity needs no separate telehealth registration (the compliance status file already lists this as open).

**H. Electronic acceptance.** I could not read Cybercrimes Act s.17 or any case on click-wrap. The Evidence Act 2011 as amended in 2023 (source 15) treats electronic records as admissible and recognises digital signatures, but the article did not address a simple tick-box. Counsel to confirm a tick-box with a stored version, timestamp, account and IP evidence is enough, and whether the two-step acceptance of the three consents should stay.

**I. Organisation-paid Memberships.** Who owns the 21-day right, who receives the refund, and what the member is told if the organisation exercises it. The draft gives it to the payer. An employee who loses access because an employer asked for a refund may need notice and the grace period. Counsel to decide, and to confirm whether a separate agreement is needed with the organisation (cover memo Question 9).

**J. Price and inclusion changes during the year; pro-rata of the 14-day grace.** The draft says price changes never touch a purchase already made. Confirm that the 14-day grace is a bonus and cannot be read as a term that shortens an annual contract.

**K. Second opinion (5,000 naira).** Sold to Members only. Confirm there is no issue with a service that is only offered to one group, and that its separate price is shown before the Member confirms.

## 4. Points that are decisions for the owner, not law

- Whether a blood test or check already taken inside the 21 days is deducted from the refund. Default draft: no deduction.
- The value of each part of the Membership, so a pro-rata refund can be worked out in advance.
- The verified emergency number to show, the support address for refund requests, and the proposed service times (5 working days to decide, 5 working days to pay, 2 and 10 working days for complaints).
- The Free plan sentence is a business-model decision made by the CMO (decision C2 in `docs/v5-sessions/00-AMENDMENTS-2026-10-08-two-tier.md`); the legal point is only whether the notice is clear.
